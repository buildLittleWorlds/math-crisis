AI is getting better at math. Why aren’t all mathematicians celebrating?

It sounds like a simple win: a machine solves a hard problem. But an answer and an understanding aren’t quite the same thing.

AI, over coffee · September 21, 2026 · 6 min read

The one-minute version

AI can help find mathematical answers faster. Some mathematicians worry that understanding, teaching, and careful explanation aren’t keeping up. Others argue that more discoveries could benefit everyone—even if the profession has to change. The hard question is what, exactly, deserves to slow down.

Imagine a group of friends working on a huge puzzle. Along the way, they learn tricks, teach newcomers, and notice interesting things they weren’t looking for. Then a machine finishes it overnight.

Is that a wonderful shortcut? Or has something valuable disappeared?

Now add a complication: what if finishing the puzzle could help people outside that room? Keeping it unfinished for the group’s benefit suddenly seems harder to defend.

That’s an imperfect analogy, but it gets us close to a surprisingly human argument happening in mathematics.

First, what happened?

On September 8, OpenAI announced a proposed solution to a famous problem about the equations describing moving fluids, called the Navier–Stokes problem. It’s one of the mathematical challenges associated with a million-dollar prize.

You don’t need to understand the equations to follow this story. What matters is that a major AI-assisted claim arrived in a field where difficult problems can shape generations of work. The prize institution, Clay, responded with interest and said its evaluation process would take time. An announcement is not the same as an awarded prize.

Then 25 initial Fields Medallist signatories—winners of one of mathematics’ highest honors—issued a statement warning that the companies’ race to solve problems could work against the mathematical community’s deeper goals.

One of them was Terence Tao. A clip of him calling for a slowdown circulated over the September 19–20 weekend. The longer exchange concerns AI-generated training material and uncertainty about rapid scaling. The remark is real in the recording’s captions; it doesn’t lay out a plan to halt every kind of AI research.

Why wouldn’t everyone want a faster answer?

Because finding the answer is only one part of what mathematicians do. They also work out why it makes sense, how to explain it, and where else the idea might be useful.

Think of getting a recipe that produces a beautiful cake, but gives you no sense of why the steps work. You might be able to follow it. Changing it for different ingredients—or teaching someone to bake—could still be difficult.

Mathematician Henry Cohn argues that poorly explained AI proofs can leave everyone else with a pile of work. Someone still has to make the ideas understandable and useful. That work takes time, and often receives less recognition than the initial discovery.

This is the strongest version of the concern: a healthy community of experts is part of how discoveries turn into benefits for other people.

But what about everyone outside that room?

There’s a strong objection here, too. If faster discovery can produce useful tools, why should the rest of society wait for a profession to feel comfortable?

Mathematician Timothy Gowers didn’t sign the statement. One of his arguments is that even if people can’t absorb every new result, a larger supply might still leave us understanding more valuable mathematics than before.

Economist Tyler Cowen suggests that universities can change what they reward. If explaining discoveries matters, give people recognition and support for doing it—even when a machine found the answer first.

The case for more care

Fast answers could weaken the learning and expertise that make knowledge useful.

The case for more speed

Delaying useful discoveries could cost everyone opportunities, simply to preserve familiar ways of working.

Neither argument settles the issue. A mathematical breakthrough isn’t automatically a new medicine or a better solar panel. But protecting academic prestige isn’t automatically a public good, either.

There’s more than one speed setting

“Slow down” can mean very different things: delay more powerful AI systems, be selective about which problems to automate, or require better explanations before a result gets scholarly credit.

Those choices have different consequences. A classroom can protect time for students to think without requiring the whole world to stop discovering things.

In an essay called “Fast math/slow math,” Ben Antieau proposes room for both ambitious AI-assisted research and slower human understanding. It’s on Tao’s blog, but Antieau wrote it—a useful detail that gets lost when posts are shared.

Tao himself is also helping to build open AI tools for mathematics. So this isn’t a clean division between people who like technology and people who don’t. It’s an argument about what the technology should help us accomplish.

A way to think about it · editorial perspective

Protect the learning.
Keep the possibilities.

Preserving someone’s status alone is a weak reason to delay a useful discovery. Preserving people’s ability to understand, question, and apply discoveries is a much stronger concern.

A promising response would support both: keep pursuing useful results, and put real resources into the people who explain, check, and teach them. Whether that can keep pace with AI is still an open question.

And that’s why this story reaches beyond mathematics. Whenever AI can produce something for us, we face a familiar choice: which effort is a chore we’re happy to lose, and which effort helps us become the person we want to be?

A question for the coffee table

What’s something you’d gladly let AI do for you—and something you’d still want to learn for yourself?
